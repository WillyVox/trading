<p>The cheapest-looking exchange isn't always the cheapest way to actually buy crypto. Two providers can advertise similar headline trading fees and still leave you paying very different amounts once you account for how you fund the account, whether you use an order book or an instant-buy screen, and what it costs to get money back out. Australians choosing an exchange in 2026 also have a genuinely new factor to weigh: AUSTRAC's expanded virtual asset service provider (VASP) rules, which changed who has to register and made that register public for the first time.</p>
<p>This guide walks through the things worth checking before you open an account — cost, AUD funding, regulation and security — without ranking one exchange as universally "best."</p>
<h2>Start with how you plan to use it</h2>
<p>A casual buyer making occasional AUD purchases has different priorities from someone who wants an order book, API access or a wide range of altcoins. If you mainly want to buy and hold Bitcoin with AUD, a simple instant-buy interface with free AUD deposits matters more than deep liquidity or advanced charting. If you expect to trade more actively, the maker/taker fee schedule and available order types matter a lot more than they do for a buy-and-hold approach.</p>
<h2>Compare the real cost, not just the headline fee</h2>
<p>Most Australian exchanges publish more than one fee. CoinSpot, for example, charges 0.1% on its order-book "Markets" product but 1% plus spread on its Instant Buy/Sell/Swap screen — two very different costs for buying the same coin on the same exchange. Swyftx uses a single tiered trading fee that starts at 0.6% and falls toward 0.1% as your rolling 30-day volume increases, with a separate spread built into the quoted price. BTC Markets tiers its exchange fee from 0.85% down to 0.10% by volume, and prices its simpler "Simple Trade" screen entirely through a variable spread rather than a separate fee line.</p>
<p>The practical lesson: an order-book product is usually cheaper than an instant-buy or "simple" product on the same exchange, but it asks more of a first-time user. Check both numbers, not just whichever one appears first on the pricing page, and re-check them close to when you actually sign up — fee schedules do change.</p>
<h2>Check AUD deposit and withdrawal options</h2>
<p>Funding method affects both cost and speed. CoinSpot, Swyftx and BTC Markets all support PayID for near-instant AUD deposits, and Swyftx and BTC Markets both confirm fee-free AUD deposits via PayID or standard bank transfer on their own fee pages. Card deposits are usually faster but can carry an extra card-processing fee that a bank transfer avoids — worth comparing if you want funds available immediately.</p>
<h2>Understand AUSTRAC registration and regulation</h2>
<p>Since 31 March 2026, AUSTRAC's registration regime for Australian crypto businesses has moved from the older "digital currency exchange" definition to the broader "virtual asset service provider" (VASP) category, capturing more business models than before. On 30 June 2026, AUSTRAC made its VASP register public, so anyone can now check whether a provider is actually registered before using it.</p>
<blockquote><p>AUSTRAC registration is an anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorism-financing requirement. It is not an ASIC financial-product licence, and it is not a government guarantee that an exchange is safe or that your money is protected if something goes wrong.</p></blockquote>
<p>Separately, ASIC has been running its own transition for crypto businesses whose products or services count as financial products under existing law — worth understanding on its own terms rather than assuming AUSTRAC registration covers it (see our companion article on what AUSTRAC registration actually means).</p>
<h2>Look at security and custody</h2>
<p>Ask who actually controls the crypto while it sits on the exchange, what proportion is kept in cold storage, and whether two-factor authentication is mandatory or optional. BTC Markets and Independent Reserve both publicly describe cold-storage and 2FA practices; Swyftx does the same. None of this eliminates risk — exchanges can still be hacked, mismanaged or become insolvent — but it's a meaningfully different risk profile from self-custody, where you (not the exchange) are responsible for keeping your keys safe. See our guide on wallets vs exchanges if you're unsure which situation you're actually choosing between.</p>

<p>CoinSpot and Swyftx both lean toward a beginner-friendly, AUD-first experience; Independent Reserve and BTC Markets both offer an order-book style interface that may suit someone comfortable with limit orders; Kraken is a global exchange some Australians use for its wider product range, though we haven't independently verified its current AU-specific fees and features for this article.</p>
<h2>Your beginner checklist before opening an account</h2>
<ul>
<li>Check the provider on the AUSTRAC VASP register</li>
<li>Compare the order-book fee and the instant-buy fee, not just one of them</li>
<li>Confirm your preferred AUD deposit/withdrawal method and whether it's free</li>
<li>Check the crypto withdrawal (network) fee for the assets you plan to hold</li>
<li>Enable 2FA as soon as the account is set up</li>
<li>Understand who actually custodies your crypto while it's on the exchange</li>
<li>Confirm the exchange lists the specific coins you actually want</li>
</ul>
