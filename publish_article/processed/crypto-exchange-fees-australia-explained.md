<p>Ask three different Australians what a given exchange "charges" and you may get three different answers — because most exchanges charge in more than one way, and it's easy to compare the wrong two numbers. This guide breaks the real cost of buying crypto into its actual parts.</p>
<h2>Trading fee vs spread — they're not the same thing</h2>
<p>A trading fee is an explicit percentage charged on the transaction. A spread is the gap between the price you're quoted and the underlying market price, built into what you see on screen rather than itemised separately. An exchange can advertise a low trading fee while still building in a wider spread on its instant-buy screen — you end up paying more than the headline number suggests, without any single line item saying so.</p>
<h2>Maker vs taker fees</h2>
<p>On an order-book product, a "maker" places an order that sits on the book (e.g. a limit order not immediately matched), while a "taker" fills an existing order immediately (e.g. a market order). Exchanges often charge takers more than makers, since takers consume existing liquidity. BTC Markets, for example, publishes a small maker rebate and a separate taker fee on its BTC pairs, alongside a distinct, higher-tier trading fee schedule on its main exchange product.</p>
<h2>Instant-buy / simple-trade premiums</h2>
<p>Instant-buy screens exist for convenience — one tap, no order book to understand — and that convenience is usually priced in. CoinSpot's own fee page shows this directly: 0.1% on its order-book "Markets" product versus 1% plus spread on Instant Buy/Sell/Swap. BTC Markets' "Simple Trade" screen works similarly, pricing entirely through a variable spread rather than a separate percentage fee. Neither is wrong — but they're not the same product, and comparing an instant-buy fee on one exchange to an order-book fee on another isn't a fair comparison.</p>
<h2>AUD deposit and withdrawal fees</h2>
<p>PayID deposits are commonly free — both Swyftx and BTC Markets confirm this on their own fee pages — while card deposits often carry an extra processing fee in exchange for near-instant availability. AUD withdrawal costs and processing times also vary by provider and method, so it's worth checking both directions, not just the deposit side.</p>
<h2>Crypto withdrawal (network) fees</h2>
<p>Moving crypto off an exchange to your own wallet usually incurs a network fee, which is largely a function of the blockchain itself rather than the exchange's own pricing (though some exchanges add a margin on top). This is separate from AUD withdrawal fees and varies by asset — Bitcoin network fees behave differently from an ERC-20 token, for instance.</p>

<h2>How to actually compare two exchanges</h2>
<p>Work out which product you'd realistically use — order book or instant-buy — and compare that specific fee, not whichever number is easiest to find. Add the AUD funding method you'd actually use. If you plan to withdraw crypto to your own wallet, factor in the network fee for that specific asset too. It's a few extra minutes of reading fee pages, but it's the only way to know what you'd actually pay.</p>
